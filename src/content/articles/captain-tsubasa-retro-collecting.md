Ask a footballer from Spain, Italy, Brazil, Japan or Thailand what made them fall in love with the game as a kid, and a surprising number will give you the same answer: *Captain Tsubasa*. Yoichi Takahashi's football manga has been running since 1981, shaped multiple World Cup generations of actual professional players, and built one of the most international fanbases in manga history. That global, cross-generation devotion is exactly what makes Tsubasa collecting so rewarding — and so evergreen.

## Why Captain Tsubasa is a collector's franchise

Most sports manga stay domestic. Tsubasa went global decades before streaming — broadcast across Europe, Latin America, the Middle East and Southeast Asia under names like *Holly e Benji*, *Oliver y Benji*, and *Flash Kicker*. The result: adults on four continents with childhood attachment to the same characters, all looking back at Japan as the source of the authentic goods.

For collectors, that means demand for original Japanese items is broad, stable, and refreshed every time a new anime adaptation or game reminds the world it exists.

## The retro games: where the legend lives

The crown jewels for game collectors are Tecmo's Famicom-era Captain Tsubasa titles — famous for inventing the "cinematic football" style where matches play out through dramatic command-based cutscenes rather than real-time action. They're beloved enough that their game system still gets homaged by modern developers.

What retro importers should know:

- **Famicom cartridges are Japan-only originals.** These games never had western releases, so the Japanese cartridge *is* the authentic artifact — no regional variant confusion.
- **Condition drives everything.** Complete-in-box copies with manuals command far more than loose carts, and boxes from the late 80s survive poorly — which is exactly why clean copies hold value.
- **Famicom hardware is region-simple.** A Famicom or an inexpensive adapter setup plays them without the region-lock drama that plagues later consoles — see our [region-free gaming guide](/guides/region-free-gaming-explained) for the details.
- **Auction listings need proxy services.** The deepest supply of retro Tsubasa games lives on Japanese auction and marketplace sites — our [proxy shopping guide](/guides/proxy-shopping-services-explained) covers when that route makes sense versus buying from exporters' retro stock.

## Beyond the games

The wider Tsubasa collecting field is generous: manga volumes (the original tankōbon runs, plus deluxe re-releases), scale figures of Tsubasa and Hyuga released over the years, stadium-scene dioramas, and anniversary merchandise that Japan produces every time a milestone rolls around. Football-club collaboration items — where Tsubasa art crosses over with real clubs — are a uniquely Tsubasa phenomenon and disappear fast when released.

Modern releases matter too: newer console titles get Asian-region English versions that are easily importable, making them a comfortable entry point before you dive into the Famicom deep end.

## Importing Tsubasa items sensibly

Retro items are one-of-a-kind purchases, so the discipline is different from pre-ordering new figures. Verify photos of the actual item, favor sellers with collector track records, and always know your landed cost before bidding — auction wins plus proxy fees plus shipping add up quickly. Captain Tsubasa taught half the world to love football. Forty years on, the artifacts of that story — the carts, the volumes, the figures — are still moving between generations. That's about as evergreen as collecting gets.
