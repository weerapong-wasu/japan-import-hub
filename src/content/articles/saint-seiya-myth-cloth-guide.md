Some figure lines are products; a few become institutions. Bandai's **Saint Seiya Myth Cloth** line — running continuously since 2003 — is firmly the second kind. Metal-plated armor pieces, fabric capes, swappable everything, and a fanbase that spans Japan, France, Italy, Spain, Brazil, Mexico and most of Asia thanks to the anime's massive international broadcasts in the 80s and 90s. If Captain Tsubasa taught the world football, Saint Seiya taught it cosmic melodrama in golden armor — and its collectors are famously devoted.

## What makes Myth Cloth different

Most action figures are plastic. Myth Cloth figures wear **die-cast metal armor** that you assemble onto the figure piece by piece — the weight, the shine, and the ritual of "dressing" a Saint are the whole point. The line's engineering has evolved through distinct generations:

| Version | What it means |
|---|---|
| Original Myth Cloth (2003–) | The classic line that started it all |
| Myth Cloth EX (2011–) | Larger, re-engineered, the current standard for Gold Saints |
| Revival editions | Modern re-releases of early figures with updated joints |
| Appendix / accessory sets | Armor display frames and upgrade parts |

The **Gold Saints in EX form** are the line's crown jewels — twelve zodiac warriors whose armors convert into "object" (totem) form. Completing all twelve is the defining long-term quest of Myth Cloth collecting, and yes, people plan years around it.

## The import reality: this line lives in Japan and Asia

Myth Cloth is produced for the Japanese and Asian markets first. Western distribution has always been thin and inconsistent, while **Tamashii Web exclusives** — often the most desired characters and armor variants — are sold through Bandai's Japanese premium channels only. Asian editions of many releases exist as well, sold through official Asian retail with their own packaging.

Practical consequence: nearly every serious Myth Cloth collector outside Japan is an importer, whether they planned to be or not. Established Japanese exporters carry current releases and pre-orders; older and exclusive pieces circulate through Japanese auction sites, which is where a [proxy service](/guides/proxy-shopping-services-explained) earns its keep.

## Bootlegs and the die-cast difference

Counterfeit Myth Cloth exists, but the die-cast construction actually helps collectors: fakes cheap out on metal content, and the weight difference is hard to disguise. Even so, sealed-box aftermarket purchases deserve the full checklist from our [bootleg guide](/guides/spotting-bootleg-anime-figures) — and for a line this assembly-heavy, missing tiny armor pieces in "used, complete" listings are as much a risk as outright fakes. ## Collecting strategy for a twenty-year line

- **Pick a Cloth generation and stick to it.** Mixing original-line and EX figures on one shelf bothers more collectors than it should — the scale and finish differ.
- **Gold Saints EX is the canonical goal**, but the Bronze protagonists in their final armor forms are the affordable, emotionally central alternative.
- **Watch for revival announcements.** Bandai re-releases beloved figures on a slow cycle; a revival announcement instantly deflates aftermarket prices on the old version — good news for buyers, cautionary for aftermarket splurges.
- **Budget for the metal.** Die-cast means heavier shipping weight than PVC figures of similar size. Run every order through our [Landed Cost Calculator](/calculator) — armor is glorious, but it weighs what it weighs.

Two decades in, Myth Cloth shows no sign of ending — the zodiac keeps turning, revivals keep coming, and a fanbase raised on four continents keeps buying. That's evergreen collecting in its purest form: enter any year, and the line will still be there growing with you.
